Nothing ruins a backpacking trip faster than a soaked sleeping bag or a waterlogged spare layer. When unexpected weather hits, your primary line of defense is how well you seal your gear. While many modern packs feature water-resistant fabrics, sustained rain or heavy road spray will eventually find its way through zippers, seams, and hydration ports.

Choosing the right pack cover means balancing fit, security, and material durability. A cover that flaps wildly in the wind or slips off when brushing against branches is worse than useless. You have to match the cover’s volume to your specific loadout—a fully stuffed winter bag will require a much larger cover than a compressed summer daypack, even if the internal liter capacity is technically the same.

Some hikers prefer the external shedding capability of a traditional cover, while others rely entirely on heavy-duty internal liners to guarantee their down jackets stay dry. Most experienced backpackers actually combine both methods for redundancy. Whether you are outfitting for a weekend trail loop or protecting your electronics during a wet urban commute, this guide breaks down the performance signals of the most popular covers and liners available right now.

## Buying Guide

### External Covers vs. Internal Liners
A traditional rain cover wraps around the outside of your pack, shedding water before it soaks into the heavy backpack material. This keeps your bag lightweight and prevents the fabric from holding onto moisture. However, external covers leave the harness and back panel exposed, meaning water can eventually seep into the main compartment from the back. Internal plastic liners (like the 5.5 Mil bags featured in this list) sit inside the main compartment. They offer superior protection against actual gear soaking, even if the pack itself gets wet. For serious downpours, layering an external cover over your [Best Hiking Backpacks](/camping-hiking/best-hiking-backpacks/) while using an internal liner for your sleeping bag provides the highest security.

### Securing Straps and Fit
The biggest buyer mistake is purchasing a cover without adjustable retention straps. Wind is a cover’s worst enemy. Look for products with cross-buckle straps or horizontal retention cords that run behind the backpack's shoulder straps. If a cover only uses an elastic rim, it will likely pop off if it snags on a tree branch or catches a strong gust of wind on an exposed ridge.

### Drainage and Water Pooling
Water obeys gravity. If rain runs down your pack and collects at the bottom of the cover, it can form a heavy puddle that slowly soaks through the bottom of your bag. Premium designs sometimes feature a small metal grommet or mesh drainage hole at the lowest point. If the cover you choose lacks this, make sure it fits snugly enough underneath the pack to prevent pooling.

### Material Denier and Visibility
Thicker fabrics (like 210D or 210T nylon) resist tearing against rocks and thorns much better than thin, ultralight options. If you frequently bushwhack, prioritize denier over weight savings. Additionally, bright colors or reflective strips are highly recommended if you walk along roads, use [Casual Daypacks](/camping-hiking/best-casual-daypacks/) for commuting, or hike during hunting season. A high-visibility cover acts as a massive safety signal to drivers and hunters.

## Common Questions Before Buying

### Do backpack rain covers keep everything dry?

They help with rain on the outside of the pack, but water can still run down your back panel or straps. Use dry bags for critical items.

### How should a pack cover fit?

It should wrap the loaded pack without overstretching and stay tight in wind. Oversized covers can flap, snag, or collect water.

### Are bright pack covers useful?

Yes for visibility in rain, roadside walking, or low light, though color matters less than secure fit and durable fabric.

## Final Summary

Securing your gear against the elements comes down to how you plan to travel. If you are bike commuting or walking along foggy roads, a high-visibility option with reflective strips is the safest choice to ensure drivers see you. If your primary risk is brushing against wet foliage on a tight trail, an external cover made from dense denier nylon will deflect moisture before it ever touches your pack. However, if you are expecting days of relentless downpours or face the risk of dropping your bag during a river crossing, relying solely on an external cover is risky. In those scenarios, utilizing a 5.5 mil internal liner to protect your sleeping bag and dry clothes provides a reliable, waterproof failsafe that external covers simply cannot match.
